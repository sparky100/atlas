# Atlas: product, performance and UX assessment

10 November 2026 · Repository revision `ab6c6a0`

**Assessment:** Atlas has a credible position as a focused business/application architecture and change-decision tool for small architecture teams. Its best opportunity is to make trusted architectural decisions easier to reach, approve and revisit. Expanding into data architecture or underlying infrastructure is unnecessary for that position.

This review covers the current local source, selected existing tests and design documents, a fresh production frontend build, read-only synthetic engine benchmarks, and competitors' official documentation. The local folder contains pre-existing untracked files; these were preserved. Application source was not changed. Browser inspection stopped because the browser tool could not reliably verify the current page address. Consequently, UX observations below are implementation-based findings and hypotheses, not results from watching users or inspecting rendered screens. No production database was queried or modified, and no live load test or full test suite was run.

**1. Competitive position and product opportunities**

The relevant comparison is capability mapping, application portfolios, dependencies, future states, decision support, governance and stakeholder engagement. Data models, lineage, networks, servers and deployment infrastructure are deliberately outside this assessment's gap list. Maintaining software services and interfaces at the application boundary remains compatible with that exclusion.

Atlas already implements more than its October competitive review acknowledges: saved reports, frozen report copies, dashboards and standard dashboard templates, Ask Atlas, a local read-only MCP server, application dispositions, cost bands, projects, decisions, value streams, and confirmation that recorded information is still accurate. These should be strengthened, not proposed as entirely new features.

| Competitor | Relevant demonstrated capabilities | Implication and opportunity for Atlas |
|---|---|---|
| SAP LeanIX | Application inventory, discovery, stakeholder enrichment and portfolio decision support. | Compete on speed from evidence to a defensible decision. Strengthen repeatable onboarding, portfolio review cycles and maintenance of application facts. Avoid an unsupported claim that Atlas is cheaper: no commercial comparison was performed. |
| Ardoq | Scenario/mainline merging, stakeholder surveys and approval of survey changes before they update the repository. | Review and scenario merging are not exclusive to Atlas. Demonstrate the quality of per-fact evidence, understandable conflicts and the complete decision/change workflow. Owner engagement remains an important gap. |
| Bizzdesign, including Horizzon/Hopex/Alfabet | Standards-based modelling, future-state scenarios, roadmaps and architecture governance within a broader suite. | Offer a smaller, more opinionated workflow with less setup, then prove that advantage through task-based trials. Selective exchange and links to established repositories can reduce adoption friction without reproducing the whole suite. |
| OrbusInfinity | Microsoft-oriented collaboration, reporting and integrations, including Outlook approvals, Power BI, Jira, SharePoint and Teams. | Meet stakeholders in existing tools. A small number of dependable integrations and a simple owner review experience could matter more than additional diagram types. |
| Sparx Enterprise Architect | Repository baselines, comparison and restoration, alongside its extensive modelling environment. | Win on approachable browser workflows and sharing evidence with non-modellers. Do not equate Sparx with a simple drawing tool or claim it has no history/change facilities. |
| Structurizr | Model-driven views, architecture decision records and workspace branches. | Atlas's opportunity is business capabilities, application-portfolio decisions and stakeholder participation without requiring a code workflow. Model-derived diagrams alone are not a differentiator. |

The October assessment's claims that Ardoq has no merge or approval workflow, and that no incumbent has comparable change facilities, should be retired. Likewise, provenance attached to a fact does not guarantee that the fact is true. Atlas can credibly promise traceability; unique superiority needs a direct workflow comparison.

**Strengthen what already works**

| Existing strength | How to strengthen it | Useful acceptance measure |
|---|---|---|
| Per-fact provenance, source locators, supersession and attestations | Show source, assertion date, last verification and accountable owner together. Separate extraction confidence from human confirmation. Make conflicting evidence and failed history loads explicit. | A first-time reader can explain the source and verification state of a critical fact in under 30 seconds. |
| Proposed changes, branches, merge/conflict handling and decisions | Present a business-language journey: identify problem → compare options → approve → deliver → confirm outcome. Preserve the distinction between approving a plan and recording something that has actually happened. | A service owner can review an option without understanding commits or branches; no ambiguity about current versus planned state. |
| Capability maps, value streams and derived diagrams | Add task-focused presets such as critical capability exposure, weak fit, missing ownership and planned retirement. Connect each finding directly to its systems, accountable owner and existing decision/project. | A director answers “what supports this capability and what are we doing about its weakness?” within the existing 60-second target. |
| Portfolio assessment, cost bands and dispositions | Explain why each candidate was flagged, distinguish duplication from justified parallel support, and carry the assessment into an option decision. Keep unknown cost visibly unknown. | Every recommendation is traceable to recorded facts; ordinal cost bands are never summed into invented savings. |
| Saved reports, dashboards, frozen copies and exports | Extend the current templates into dated executive briefings with conclusions, next actions and evidence coverage. Add snapshot comparisons and scenario comparisons. | A briefing can be reproduced later and every actionable row opens its underlying record. |
| Capture, sector maps and duplicate handling | Package a guided import with mapping preview, rejection reasons, repeat-import reconciliation and a clear completion summary. Use sector maps to accelerate initial value. | Time to first useful capability/application assessment; correction rate and duplicate rate after import. |
| Ask Atlas and read-only MCP | Add task-specific prompts, explicit model/state stamps, coverage limits and an opt-in way to save an answer as an evidence-linked briefing. Expand evaluation around missing facts and conflicting evidence. | Track answer grounding, correct refusal/qualification and first-use task completion, not just fluent responses. |

The fixed taxonomy is an advantage if it reduces training and inconsistent modelling. Custom attributes already offer controlled extensibility. Add coherent sector attribute/report packs before considering arbitrary new element types.

**2. Performance findings and improvements**

The most serious issue is the cost and reliability of handling a long-lived model, not just drawing a large diagram.

**Fresh measurements.** The frontend production build (`pnpm exec vite build`) passed. Its main JavaScript file is **2,127.58 kB minified / 604.92 kB gzip**, and the main CSS is **166.51 kB / 24.96 kB gzip**. These are build output sizes, not measured network transfer or browser parse times. The build warns about oversized chunks. It does not establish that the full release package, database or edge functions have been verified.

The synthetic engine benchmark ran in **Node 22.12.0 on Windows**, in the current workstation environment. It performs no database or browser operations. Portfolio timings are the median of three measured runs after one warm-up. Each system supports three capabilities; capability count is one tenth of system count. There are no ownership edges or containers in that fixture, so it is not a complete enterprise workload.

| Engine operation | Size | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Replay linear history | 500 commits | Completed; final fact verified |
| Replay linear history | 2,000 commits | Completed; final fact verified |
| Replay linear history | 5,000 commits | `RangeError: Maximum call stack size exceeded` |
| Replay linear history | 10,000 and 20,000 commits | Same failure |
| Build portfolio rows | 200 systems / 600 support edges | **33.7 ms** |
| Build portfolio rows | 2,000 systems / 6,000 support edges | **1,468.6 ms** |
| Build portfolio rows | 10,000 systems / 30,000 support edges | **37,900.4 ms** |

The history fixture repeatedly updates one fact, deliberately isolating history depth from model size. The exact stack limit varies by runtime; 5,000 is a reproduction point here, not a universal product limit. Portfolio timings exclude retrieval, React rendering and layout, so they must not be quoted as page-load measurements.

Reproduction: [benchmark](C:/Users/parki/OneDrive/Documents/Projects/atlas/scripts/benchmarkAudit.ts), [results](C:/Users/parki/OneDrive/Documents/Projects/atlas/output/audit/benchmark-results.json).

| Priority | Finding and effect | Recommended improvement |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | History replay recursively walks parents and can overflow. Ordinary saves call the history loader and full replay. A small, old model can therefore become difficult or impossible to edit through this path. | Replace recursive traversal with an explicit stack while preserving merge ordering. Use verified checkpoints and replay only the necessary ancestry since the checkpoint. A checkpoint must preserve the retracted/history information required by restores, not just visible rows. |
| P1 | Every `commitToBranch` loads all model commits and deltas, including history that may not be relevant to that branch. This adds growing transfer, parsing and validation cost to each edit. | Reuse the immutable validated head for the expected commit; load missing ancestry incrementally. Keep server-side concurrency and permission checks, append-only evidence and restore semantics intact. The existing incremental database write path should be retained: the issue is not that every ordinary save sends a whole projection. |
| P1 | Portfolio construction repeatedly scans support and ownership edges for each element and repeatedly recomputes supporter groups. The measured calculation becomes too slow for interactive use. | Build maps indexed by source element, target capability and owner once. Cache per-capability supporter bands and derive row summaries from them. Fetch/expand full alternative-supporter lists only when needed. Use a worker for remaining expensive work after reducing the work itself. |
| P1 | The route tree imports every page eagerly. An eager element-export path also imports XLSX. This contributes to the large initial bundle. | Split at route boundaries and load the element spreadsheet exporter on demand. Keep the shell and common record view small; prefetch likely next routes. Some PDF and report exports are already lazy, so extend the existing approach. |
| P2 | Several large page datasets use component-local effects rather than the shared query cache. Navigation can reload identical commit-scoped elements/edges; cold branch base/head loads can each download the full history. | Share model reads by authorised user/organisation, model, commit and dataset; deduplicate in-flight requests. Clear protected caches on account/access changes. Share a history-loader fetch across cold base/head calculations. |
| P2 | Browse and report tables render every matching row; mobile cards also map the full result. Filtering/sorting runs on the main thread. | Accessible windowing or pagination, stable keys and deferred search for large collections. Bound the displayed result independently of full export. Preserve keyboard editing and screen-reader context. |
| P2 | `selectAll` retrieves sequential offset pages using exact-count queries; an exact page multiple triggers an extra empty request. | Stop once the known complete count is reached. For large stable datasets, use keyset pagination and count once, retaining completeness checks. Move selective filters/aggregations server-side where useful rather than lifting the 50-ID safety bound. Measure query plans before adding indexes. |
| P2 | Ask tools cap returned answers but some first accesses load all model elements, relationships and aliases. A 50-row output cap is not a 50-row query. | Use bounded server queries for common lookup/filter tasks, while retaining one authorised, commit-stamped view. Measure time-to-first-answer and tool costs separately from provider latency. |

**Existing optimisation worth preserving:** worker-based landscape layout, viewport culling, cached immutable branch heads, shared metadata queries, incremental ordinary database writes, commit-result cache seeding, lazy provenance histories and dashboard data loaded once per source rather than once per tile. Replacing the rendering framework or database is not supported by these findings.

**Older measurements are warnings, not fresh results.** The September acceptance report records relationship creation at 1,433 ms, an attribute save at 1,335 ms, and system creation at 699 ms against a 500 ms target on a 20-system fixture. Four recorded routes also miss 300 ms. These results should be rerun against the current build before claiming present-day latency. The landscape test's actual low-tail frame-rate gate is 28 fps even though its title mentions a 45 fps target. Passing that test would not demonstrate the advertised target. [Recorded timings](C:/Users/parki/OneDrive/Documents/Projects/atlas/docs/perf/2026-09-acceptance.md), [actual frame gates](C:/Users/parki/OneDrive/Documents/Projects/atlas/e2e/landscape-perf.engine.spec.ts:252).

A useful performance matrix crosses **model size** (200, 2,000 and 10,000 systems) with **history depth** (short, 5,000 and 20,000 commits), sparse/dense dependencies, cold/warm navigation and multiple users. Measure save-to-confirmed-display, first useful content, first usable diagram, input responsiveness, memory and p95 frame time on a representative laptop and realistic network. Keep synthetic engine measurements separate from end-to-end measurements. Add automated regression gates to the release workflow; current documentation says CI deploys but relies on local verification.

**3. UX and UI assessment**

**What works in the implementation**

- Overview versus My work reflects two real jobs: understanding business exposure and maintaining the model. Both should remain available without making either a permission boundary.
- Contextual links from findings into decisions/projects reduce the distance from diagnosis to action. This is more valuable than a large library of disconnected diagrams.
- One model behind multiple views, deep links and retained state make the product coherent and shareable. Clear unknown/missing states are preferable to silently treating absence as a healthy result.
- The shared token system, light/dark themes, density settings and consistent controls are sound foundations for visual consistency.
- Mobile cards/lists, the five-item bottom bar, keyboard search, semantic table markup and colour-independent signals show accessibility intent. They do not by themselves prove accessibility compliance.
- Standard dashboards, chart-to-report drill-down, presentation mode and exports already exist. The opportunity is to improve their narrative and complete the drill-down journey.

**What is weak or needs validation**

| Issue | Evidence / consequence | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Too many competing destinations | The rail has 15 destinations. Home contains Overview/My work while the Work group also has Work overview, Projects, Changes and Decisions. Those distinctions make sense internally but may be hard for an occasional user. | Test task-based labels and a smaller default navigation for occasional users. Explain project = delivery effort, decision = choice and rationale, change = proposed model alteration. Keep advanced destinations discoverable. |
| Creation is partly hidden inside search | The onboarding checklist tells users to type a name in search to create a system. That is good for duplicate avoidance but not an obvious creation affordance. | Offer explicit “Add application” and “Import inventory” actions that open the same search-first duplicate check. Guide the first complete capability → application → assessment journey. |
| Enterprise terminology leaks into state controls | Technical concepts such as `main`, ref, branch and commit coexist with business terms. Reports use main even when surrounding navigation can retain another selected ref. | Use “Current architecture”, “Proposed change” and a named saved baseline in primary UI. Show the exact state/date being analysed, and clearly announce when a page uses current architecture rather than the selected proposal. Keep technical identifiers in details. |
| Confidence risks overstating truth | The contract assigns human assertions and template seeds confidence 1.00; ConfidenceDots exposes this as “confidence 100%”. A manually entered or templated statement is not certain simply because it was entered that way. | Separate “Human asserted”, “Owner verified on date”, “Template” and “AI extraction confidence”. Show verification coverage without implying a calibrated probability of real-world truth. |
| Some meaningful text is too faint | Light-theme `--text-faint: #8a93a1` against white calculates to **3.10:1**. It is used for missing optional values as well as secondary labels; ordinary informative text needs 4.5:1 under WCAG AA. Provenance badges also use an 11 px token. | Darken informative secondary text in both themes and give evidence metadata more readable sizing. Verify actual backgrounds, focus states and 200% zoom. This is a concrete token issue, not a complete accessibility audit. |
| Provenance interaction and failure states need stronger treatment | FactBadge supports hover/focus, but has no explicit click toggle or Escape handler in that component. Failed history retrieval is converted to an empty history. Its cached chain is not keyed/reset when the fact delta changes. | Use a labelled, keyboard/touch-operable evidence popover with explicit dismissal, viewport-aware placement, retry and an “unavailable” state. Key cached history by the fact delta. Distinguish “no earlier evidence” from “could not retrieve it”. |
| Dashboard loading resembles an empty dashboard | Tiles/reports start as empty arrays; the no-tiles message is rendered before loading completes. A request failure can leave the same empty view alongside an error banner. | Separate loading, truly empty, partially loaded and failed states. Make retry local to the failed area and prevent incomplete exports from appearing complete. |
| Reporting stops one step short of action | Charts drill into reports, but the generic result table renders cell values as plain text. Users then have to search again to open an application. | Preserve record IDs separately from displayed values; make names link to the correct record/state. Add a contextual action or owner link where appropriate. |
| Some report/chart language is generic | Labels such as “Rows match” or “Rows by…” explain the query mechanism rather than its business significance. | Say “Applications awaiting assessment”, “Critical applications nearing retirement” or another precise subject. Put the conclusion first, followed by denominator, unknowns, date and action. |
| Dashboard composition has a configuration detour | To turn an ungrouped report into a chart, the user is directed to leave the dashboard and edit report settings. | Offer compatible chart choices plus an inline grouping step and preview. Retain the saved-report model behind the workflow. |
| AI work is disposable by default | Ask Atlas states that questions/results are not saved when the user leaves. This avoids unnoticed retention but loses useful research. | Offer explicit “Save as briefing” with citations and the model commit, governed by the existing sharing model. Do not silently retain conversations. |

The strongest visual direction is an evidence-led executive briefing: a clear conclusion, a few meaningful measures, affected capabilities/applications, the action being taken, and an easy route to evidence. Keep dense modelling tools for architects, but make this briefing the natural experience for occasional readers. A rendered review is still needed to judge whitespace, alignment, diagram readability, responsive clipping and overall visual polish; source inspection cannot settle those questions.

**Recommended sequence**

| Sequence | Work package | Completion evidence |
|---|---|---|
| First | Reliable scale: iterative history traversal, checkpoint strategy, indexed portfolio computation, route/export splitting. | Deep-history regression passes; portfolio calculation no longer blocks interaction at the agreed model size; measured reduction in cold-load code. |
| Next | Trust and usability: contrast, evidence status/interaction, honest loading/error states, direct report links and clear architecture-state labels. | Keyboard/touch and zoom checks; no misleading empty/history state; occasional users can identify current versus proposed architecture. |
| Then | Product value: owner review loop, evidence-linked decision briefings, relationship/findings reports and snapshot comparisons. | Owners complete reviews; directors can understand an exposure and its planned response without an architect narrating. |
| Customer-led | One or two application-level connectors, enterprise identity and selective interoperability. | A real onboarding/renewal requirement is met, with provenance and access controls preserved. |

Validate with architects and occasional service owners separately. Suggested tasks: understand a capability's exposure in 60 seconds; add/link an application; confirm a fact and find its source; compare two options; approve without confusing planned and current states; move from a dashboard finding to the responsible owner. Record time, errors, hesitation and assistance required. These tests should determine the next navigation and visual-design changes.
